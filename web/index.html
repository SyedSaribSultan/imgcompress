Quality is measured, not guessed

Most compressors ask you to pick a quality number and hope. This one saves each image several different ways, opens every version back up, compares it to your original, and keeps the smallest one that still looks close enough. Then it shows you the evidence.

1

Encode it several ways

mozjpeg, palette PNG, lossless PNG — plus WebP and AVIF if you allow them. Each format is searched separately for the smallest setting that still looks close enough to your original, and every PNG gets a lossless oxipng squeeze.

2

Compare every version

Each one is opened back up and compared to your original with SSIMULACRA 2 — the metric the image-compression community converged on, running right here in your browser and validated against the reference implementation. Transparency is scored on dark and light backdrops; the worse score wins.

3

Keep the smallest one that still looks right

The winner is whichever version came out smallest while still looking close enough to your original — and nothing bigger than what you started with is ever produced. Files that are already small enough are left exactly as they are.

The format is a comparison, not an assumption

Why "Design tool or document" refuses WebP

"Just use WebP" is the standard advice, and it's wrong if your images are going into Figma. Figma's plugin API only knows PNG, JPEG and GIF, and the standing community answer is that a WebP dropped onto the canvas is decoded and re-encoded as PNG — turning your beautifully compressed 40 KB WebP into a multi-megabyte PNG inside the .fig. Office suites and document editors behave much the same way. That destination therefore sticks to formats those tools store byte-for-byte, and clamps anything above 4096px — beyond that they rescale destructively on import, so the choice is between our resampling and theirs. Every other destination allows the modern formats, which is why Website or app is the default: the restriction is a fact about design tools, not about images.

Questions

Is anything uploaded?

No. Compression runs entirely in your browser using Web Workers and WebAssembly. Images never leave your device, and the page makes no network requests after it loads. There are no cookies and no analytics.

When does it avoid WebP and AVIF?

Only when you tell it the image is going into a design tool or a document. Figma's plugin API accepts PNG, JPEG and GIF, and the standing community answer is that WebP uploads are decoded and re-encoded as PNG on import — turning a small WebP into a large PNG inside the .fig file. Choosing Design tool or document sticks to the formats those tools store as they were given them. The default, Website or app, uses everything.

How is quality measured?

Every version is opened back up and compared to your original. The comparison looks at local contrast and detail the way eyes do, rather than counting pixel differences, and it can see colour damage that the older measures everybody still uses cannot. 100 means indistinguishable. Images with transparency are compared over both a dark and a light background, and the worse of the two is the one that counts — a halo you cannot see on white is still a defect.

The measure is SSIMULACRA 2, which agrees with human judgement at r≈0.88 against about 0.76 for the older SSIM. It runs entirely in your browser, checked against the reference version.

What is the difference between this and the desktop version?

They compress to the same measured SSIMULACRA 2 floor with the same class of encoders. The desktop version (pip install imgcompress) adds workflow: folder watching, batch saves straight to disk, a scriptable CLI — plus libimagequant and zopfli, which are still worth a few percent on palette images.

Which formats can it read and write?

It reads PNG, JPEG, WebP, BMP and GIF in every modern browser, and TIFF where the browser can decode it. Animated GIFs are passed through untouched. Output is JPEG, palette PNG, lossless PNG, WebP (lossy or lossless) or AVIF, depending on the target and what your browser supports.

Is it free?

Yes — free and open source under the MIT licence. The code is on GitHub.
